BTI · S01 — Number bases and representations

ASE Bucharest · CSIE · Year I, semester I · 7 seminars × 90 minutes

90 minutes2 / 10 / 168 bit

Open the interactive lesson

What we will learn

  • Distinguish value from representation; read bases and positions.
  • Convert decimal ↔ binary and check in reverse.
  • Add and subtract in binary, explaining carry and borrowing.
  • Convert binary ↔ hex in groups of four and decimal ↔ hex directly.
  • Explain the 0–255 range in 8 bits and the 9 bits required for 256.
  • Identify and correct an error in your method.

Why this will help

  • Read program output as 179₁₀, B3₁₆ or 10110011₂ and check equivalence.
  • Check an 8-bit format: 0–255 fit; 256 does not. This prepares the S05 format.
  • Locate errors using reverse conversion or a method check instead of guessing.

What to retain after the steps fade

  • The base sets the digits and positional weights.
  • Conversion preserves value; leading zeros can set the width.
  • To another base: divide, reverse remainders. To decimal: digit × weight.
  • Hex: four bits per digit. Optional octal: three.
  • Carry and borrowing preserve value.
  • 8 unsigned bits: 0–255. For 256: 9 bits.

This lesson uses non-negative integers. Signed representations and fractions require other conventions. Supplements are marked separately from the 90-minute route.

01 · In the seminar · 0–5 min

S01 · One value, different representations

Conversion changes representation, not value. Use non-negative integers: 0, 1, 2 and so on.

Why this method works

Computing uses bits; decimal and hexadecimal are easier to read. We convert between them and check the value.

Steps to follow

  1. Write the base beside the number to interpret each digit.
  2. Work by hand, then check in reverse: recover the starting value.
  3. The 90-minute route covers bases 10, 2 and 16, binary arithmetic and the 8-bit boundary, with additional reference pages.
One number, three representations

45₁₀ = 101101₂ = 2D₁₆. We will build this equality step by step.

Note your understanding of positions. Keep working if a step is unclear.

A mistake to avoid: Do not infer the base from the appearance of the string.

Start with units. S05 uses an 8-bit hexadecimal format, S06 checks an interpretation convention and S07 counts the 1 bits and determines the parity bit.

02 · In the seminar · 5–8 min

The base sets the digits and groups

In base b, digits range from 0 to b−1. Moving left multiplies each weight by b.

Why this method works

The base sets grouping: ten units make one ten in decimal; two units form a group of two in binary.

Steps to follow

  1. Read the subscript: ₂ means base 2, ₁₀ base 10 and ₁₆ base 16. It labels the number.
  2. Check the digits: binary allows only 0 and 1.
  3. 10 means one group of the base and zero units, not always ten.

03 · In the seminar · 8–12 min

Positions, powers and the role of zero

The rightmost exponent is 0, with weight b⁰ = 1. Moving left increases the exponent and multiplies the weight by the base.

Why this method works

The power sets weight; the digit counts groups. Zero holds other digits in position.

Steps to follow

  1. Write the weights from the right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Start with 1.
  2. Align all digits with weights, including zeros; keep every position.
  3. Add digit × weight. Exponent 4, power 2⁴ and value 16 are related but distinct.

04 · In the seminar · 12–18 min

Decimal → binary: divide and keep the remainder

Repeated division by 2 produces binary digits. Keep the integer quotient and remainder, 0 or 1.

Why this method works

N = 2 × quotient + remainder separates groups of two from the leftover unit. Continue with the quotient.

Steps to follow

  1. Write 19 = 2 × 9 + 1. Divide the integer quotient 9 next.
  2. Repeat with 9, 4, 2 and 1. The remainder is a result digit; divide the quotient next.
  3. Stop when the quotient is 0, then read remainders upwards.
  4. For zero, write 0 directly; do not repeat the divisions.

05 · In the seminar · 18–21 min

Why we read remainders upwards

The first remainder is the rightmost units digit. Subsequent remainders belong to larger positions on the left.

Why this method works

Even correct divisions give a wrong result if digits are rearranged. Order determines positional value.

Steps to follow

  1. In 19 = 2 × 9 + 1, the first remainder is the rightmost units digit.
  2. 9 = 2 × 4 + 1 gives 19 = 4 × 4 + 2 × 1 + 1. The second remainder has weight 2.
  3. The next remainders have weights 4, 8 and 16. We discover them from right to left.
The same digits, different values

10011₂ = 16 + 2 + 1 = 19. In the wrong order, 11001₂ = 16 + 8 + 1 = 25.

Valid digits do not ensure correct value. Mark the remainder direction.

A mistake to avoid: Reading downwards gives 25 here, instead of 19.

Checking in reverse becomes a general method.

06 · In the seminar · 21–27 min

Binary → decimal: add the contributions

The decimal value is the sum of digits multiplied by their weights: digit × power of 2.

Why this method works

A binary digit is 0 or 1: its weight contributes or does not. This also checks conversion from decimal.

Steps to follow

  1. Write weights from the right, starting at 1. For 101101₂, reach 32.
  2. Select positions containing 1: 32, 8, 4 and 1. Keep zeros aligned.
  3. Add 32 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 45; compare with the starting value.

08 · In the seminar · 33–40 min

Check the value and each zero’s position

Leading zeros preserve value. Adding a zero on the right in binary multiplies the value by 2.

Why this method works

Alignment and hexadecimal grouping depend on this. Interior and trailing zeros hold necessary positions.

09 · In the seminar · 40–43 min

Binary addition: carrying starts at two

Two units become one in the left column. Include the incoming carry from the right.

Why this method works

As in decimal, but with threshold 2: two equally sized groups become one twice that size.

Steps to follow

  1. Align units; pad the shorter operand with leading zeros if needed.
  2. Add both digits and incoming carry. The total is 0, 1, 2 or 3.
  3. Divide the total by 2: write the remainder, carry the quotient. For 3, write 1 and carry 1.

Complete addition, column by column

The final carry becomes a new digit on the left. The sum can have more digits than the operands.

Why this method works

Dropping the final carry removes a positional value. Calculate the complete sum without an imposed storage width.

11 · In the seminar · 46–49 min

Binary subtraction: borrowing is an exchange

For 0−1, exchange one unit on the left for two units in the current column.

Why this method works

Borrowing redistributes value: one unit on the left equals two here. Reduce the lending column too.

Steps to follow

  1. Align the units. Direct cases are 0−0 = 0, 1−0 = 1 and 1−1 = 0.
  2. For 0−1, find a unit on the left, reduce its column and bring two units here.
  3. Subtract after exchanging. The temporary amount 2 is not a binary result digit.

12 · In the seminar · 49–53 min

The borrowing chain passes through every zero

Each intermediate zero receives two units, passes one on and keeps one. The final column receives two to subtract from.

Why this method works

Every exchange preserves value. In 32 − 11, borrowing for units starts at weight 32.

Steps to follow

  1. Align weights 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. The only available 1 is at 32.
  2. One 32 becomes two groups of 16. Keep one and exchange the other for two groups of 8; continue to the units.
  3. Amounts become 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2. Value remains 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 2 = 32.
  4. Subtract 001011 at the same weights. Check the difference 21 using 21 + 11 = 32.

Hexadecimal shortens the representation: four bits per digit.

14 · In the seminar · 55–57 min

Hexadecimal: sixteen digits

Base 16 uses 0–9 and A–F. A–F have values 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 and 15.

Why this method works

Sixteen symbols are needed. D is one digit worth 13, not a variable or two digits.

Steps to follow

  1. In calculations, replace the letter with its value: D contributes 13 × weight.
  2. Weights are 1, 16, 256. In 2D₁₆, digit 2 has weight 16.
  3. Write uppercase A–F. Apply the familiar positional rule rather than memorising every number.

15 · In the seminar · 57–60 min

Binary → hexadecimal: groups of four from the right

One hexadecimal digit corresponds to four bits because 16 = 2⁴. The local weights are 8, 4, 2, 1.

Why this method works

Grouping compresses the representation and preserves value. Start from the right to keep the units position.

Steps to follow

  1. Take rightmost group 1101 from 101101₂, leaving 10 on the left.
  2. Pad only on the left: 10 becomes 0010. Do not add trailing zeros.
  3. 0010 means 2; 1101 means 13, written D. Join digits in the same order.

Reference · Octal uses groups of three

Octal uses digits 0–7 and three bits per digit: 8 = 2³. This page is optional.

Why this method works

Octal uses the same idea with local weights 4, 2, 1. It remains reference material outside required activities.

Steps to follow

  1. Group three bits from the right: 1011101₂ gives 1, 011, 101.
  2. Pad the first group to 001. Values 1, 3 and 5 give 135₈.
  3. In reverse, write three bits per digit: 53₈ gives 101 and 011.
  4. For 101₈, keep the interior group: 001, 000, 001. Omit only the integer’s leading zeros.

17 · In the seminar · 60–63 min

Hexadecimal → binary: keep four bits per digit

Write four bits per hex digit. Join the groups, then remove only the whole integer’s leading zeros.

Why this method works

Full groups preserve distance from units. Incomplete interior groups shift left-hand digits and change value.

Steps to follow

  1. In B3₁₆, B is worth 11 and becomes 1011; 3 becomes 0011.
  2. Join them: 10110011₂. The zeros for 3 are interior; keep them.
  3. For 6E₁₆, write 0110 and 1110. This gives 01101110₂, or the shorter form 1101110₂.

20 · In the seminar · 66–70 min

8 bits: value, width and the 255/256 boundary

8 unsigned bits give 2⁸ = 256 representations for values 0–255. The value 256 needs 9 bits.

Why this method works

Representations start at zero. Leading zeros set width; they cannot replace positions needed by larger values.

Steps to follow

  1. The eight weights sum to 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 255.
  2. 2⁷ ≤ 255 < 2⁸ needs 8 positions, from 7 to 0. 256 = 2⁸ needs a 1 and eight zeros: 9 bits.
  3. 10₁₀ = 1010₂ = A₁₆. In eight bits, write 00001010₂ = 0A₁₆: the same value.
  4. Keep eight digits when requested. For the shortest form, remove only leading zeros.

21 · Supplement for reference · Extra · outside the 90 min

Reference · Exponent, power and minimum bit count

For N > 0, if 2ᵏ ≤ N < 2ᵏ⁺¹, at least k+1 bits are needed. Exponent k differs from value 2ᵏ.

Why this method works

The highest position is k; position 0 also counts. Large examples are references without new required tasks.

Steps to follow

  1. For 200000, the neighbouring powers are 2¹⁷ = 131072 and 2¹⁸ = 262144.
  2. Distinguish the answers: exponent 17, power 131072, minimum 18 bits.
  3. 262144 = 2¹⁸ needs 19 bits; 262143 needs 18. Count digit 1 too.
  4. The rule requires N > 0. For zero, the shortest form is one digit, 0.

24 · In the seminar · 80–90 min

What to remember and how to continue

Conversion preserves value. Base, positions and width explain representations; exchanges explain arithmetic.

Why this method works

Remember methods that rebuild results: digits × weights, reversed remainders and exchanges that preserve value.

Steps to follow

  1. From decimal: divide by the target base and reverse the remainders. To decimal: add contributions.
  2. Binary ↔ hex: four bits per digit, left padding and full interior groups.
  3. Keep the final carry and every borrowing exchange. Check value or use the inverse operation.
  4. Explain for practice: why is 10₂ = 2? Why does digit 3 in B3 need 0011? Why does 255 fit in 8 bits while 256 does not?

Solve before asking for feedback. These are learning exercises, with no gradebook points. Any announced formal assessment has separate instructions.

R1 · Convert 22₁₀ to binary.

Answer and verification

10110

16 + 4 + 2 = 22. Reversed division remainders give 10110.

R2 · Convert 6D₁₆ to decimal.

Answer and verification

109

D = 13; digit 6 has weight 16: 6 × 16 + 13 = 109.

R3 · Add 1101₂ + 1011₂.

Answer and verification

11000

13 + 11 = 24. Carrying gives 11000; keep the final carry.

R4 · Subtract 100000₂ − 1101₂.

Answer and verification

10011

Align 001101 under 100000; follow the exchanges. Difference: 19. Check: 19 + 13 = 32.

R5 · What is the minimum unsigned bit count for 256₁₀?

Answer and verification

9

256 = 2⁸ = 100000000₂. A 1 and eight zeros occupy nine positions.
